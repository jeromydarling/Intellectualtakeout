We all want the best for our kids. Because of this desire, it’s quite discouraging to see when efforts to boost progress in reading, math, and other subjects flatline in schools across the country, as the [chart below](https://object.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/wp-content/uploads/coulson_nat_ed_trends_chart_sept_2014.gif) shows.

![coulson\_nat\_ed\_trends\_chart\_sept\_2014](https://chroniclesmagazine-legacy.ae-admin.com/assets/3/7/coulson_nat_ed_trends_chart_sept_2014.gif)

On the other hand, this perpetual stagnation causes us to sit up and notice when a school manages to boost its achievement in dramatic fashion.

Such is the case with Feversham Primary Academy in Great Britain. According to _[The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2017/oct/03/school-results-music-bradford),_ Feversham was a failure a few years ago. Achievement was low and seemed unlikely to improve given that many students hail from disadvantaged backgrounds or are English Language Learners.

But as _The Guardian_ explains, the school began using the “[Kodály approach](http://www.britishkodalyacademy.org/kodaly_approach.htm), which involves teaching children to learn, subconsciously at first, through playing musical games.” By teaching these musical games and encouraging memorization of classic works such as Shakespeare, the school has experienced the following change:

> “Seven years ago Feversham was in special measures and making [headlines](http://www.thetelegraphandargus.co.uk/news/7985429.Primary_school_is_now_officially__failing_/) for all the wrong reasons. Today it is rated [‘good’](https://reports.ofsted.gov.uk/inspection-reports/find-inspection-report/provider/ELS/138867) by Ofsted and is in the top 10% nationally for pupil progress in reading, writing and maths, according to the most recent [data](https://www.compare-school-performance.service.gov.uk/school/138867). In 2011, the school was 3.2 percentage points behind the national average in English. This year 74% of its pupils achieved the expected standard in reading, writing and maths, against a national average of 53%. It is 7.1 points above the average for reading and 3.4 above for writing. In maths, the school was 2.4 points behind the national average in 2011 and is now 6.5 above it. Its results for disadvantaged pupils are well above average.”

Such increases are quite impressive and appear to mirror the [40% achievement gains](/blog/studying-shakespeare-brings-school-40-jump-test-scores) another British school experienced after incorporating Shakespeare into lesson plans.  

So why is it that these simple techniques appear to produce such stellar results?

The answer to that question may be found in what music and memorization appear to do the brain. Research suggests that exposing children to music [fosters brain development](https://www.webmd.com/balance/features/5-ways-music-helps-the-mind#1) and [boosts](http://neurosciencenews.com/neuroscience-music-enchances-learning-neuroplasticity/) their “vocabulary and reading ability.” Likewise, [memorization](https://www.psychologytoday.com/blog/memory-medic/201305/memorization-is-not-dirty-word-2) “exercises” a child’s brain, training children to pay attention while also laying a foundation upon which they can build future facts and insights.

These components are core elements of [classical education](https://classicalacademicpress.com/what-is-classical-education/). In the grade school years – also known as the grammar stage – classical education capitalizes on the love children have for rote learning, using songs and rhymes to instill historical dates, scientific facts, and famous literature in their brains. When they move beyond these years, they find they have a wealth of knowledge at their fingertips from which they can draw, make connections, and spin off new thoughts.

The funny thing is, while this common sense approach to early childhood education was standard practice for centuries, it has been abandoned in recent years. Shunning rote learning, we have instead told young children to draw on their own (limited) experience or feelings when completing school assignments.

Classical education methods of music and rote learning have been experiencing a revival in many home and private schools in recent years and have enjoyed a good [deal of success](https://classicalchristian.org/measure-it/). The dramatic turnaround in the Feversham school suggests the success of these methods is not limited to those of a “privileged” status.

Is it time we ask ourselves if modern schools have been too hasty in tossing out the rote learning methods of music and memorization?
