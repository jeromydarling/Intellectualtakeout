It’s National Apprenticeship Week in England, and although the Brits are more accepting of the practice than the United States, they still deal with several misconceptions about apprenticeships. Five of these were recently addressed in [_The Guardian_](http://www.theguardian.com/careers/2016/mar/14/low-paid-antisocial-and-non-academic-common-apprenticeship-myths) and are abbreviated below:

**Myth 1: Apprenticeship students are dumb failures.**

Fact: Instead of being high school dropouts, many students must present good grades in their A-levels (exit examinations) in order to be accepted to an apprenticeship program.

**Myth 2: Apprenticeship and college are mutually exclusive.**

Fact: As _The Guardian_ explains, “If you go to university, you can still complete an apprenticeship afterwards, and vice versa.” Furthermore, many apprenticeship programs incorporate class time with work, enabling students to earn a degree while gaining valuable hands-on experience.

**Myth 3: Apprenticeships can’t be found in white collar industries.**

Fact: Although apprenticeships were once the realm of manual labor and manufacturing jobs, recent years have found them [expanding beyond that arena](/blog/good-news-popularity-apprenticeship-spreading). In fact, _The Guardian_ reports that _“‘_[business, health and retail](http://www.parliament.uk/briefing-papers/SN06113.pdf) … are the sectors with the most apprenticeships.’”

**Myth 4: Apprenticeship jobs pay too little.**

Fact: While some apprenticeship-trained jobs do have lower salaries, those who hold them do not have deal with student debt or lost productivity from their years in college. On the other hand, some apprenticeship-trained students have been [found to earn more](http://www.theguardian.com/careers/2016/mar/14/better-job-prospects-university-or-apprenticeships) than those who hold degrees from “non-elite” colleges. 

**Myth 5: Apprenticeships eliminate the college social experience.**

Fact: Diminishing college social life is not necessarily a bad thing, for it can often detract and distract from the true purpose of college: learning. However, for those still interested in having fun, the camaraderie of apprenticeship still provides many opportunities for social life.

The long and short of things is that any direction a young person takes can have pros and cons. Those who have pursued an apprenticeship, however, have found themselves to have an advantage over their college-educated peers in the areas of maturity and work-experience at a younger age. As one young apprenticeship student [noted](http://www.theguardian.com/careers/2016/mar/14/better-job-prospects-university-or-apprenticeships):

> “‘It’s interesting as we’ve had university graduates join the team too and they weren’t as confident as I had expected, meanwhile all the apprentices were thrown in at the deep end.
> 
> …
> 
> I’ve had a head start.’”

In the U.S., we’re always looking for the quickest way to give kids a head start in everything from sports to academics. But by sidelining apprenticeship opportunities, have we ignored one of the best head starts we could ever give a student?
