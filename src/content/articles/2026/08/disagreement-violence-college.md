There was a time, not too long ago, when intellectual disagreement could be had in good faith. Students were encouraged to debate an idea, defend it, and walk away sharper for having engaged in discourse. Now, for a growing share of college students, the only acceptable way to reconcile disagreement is to punch your interlocutor in the face, or [burn down a building](/2024/11/question-the-protests-on-college-campuses/) and threaten others who hold those same so-called intolerable ideas.

This shift didn’t happen overnight. Back in 2017, the social psychologist Jonathan Haidt and his coauthor Greg Lukianoff warned us in [The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/education/archive/2017/07/why-its-a-bad-idea-to-tell-students-words-are-violence/533970/) that a new idea was permeating campuses. It was the notion that speech and words had apparently been adopted into the currency of what constituted violence. Haidt and Lukianoff were, in part, responding to an essay ([“When is Speech Violence?”](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/07/14/opinion/sunday/when-is-speech-violence.html)) written by Lisa Feldman Barrett, a well-known emotion researcher at Northeastern University, who cited evidence that chronic stress can damage the brain and body.

Haidt and Lukianoff didn’t dispute Barrett’s scientific findings. Rather, they disputed the notion that being upset by the content of a college lecture was nothing like the sustained trauma that her research appeared to describe. The distinction was important, they argued, because a free society depends on intellectual disagreement. Eight years later, it appears that Haidt and Lukianoff were right to have reservations about Barrett’s position.

FIRE’s [2026 College Free Speech Rankings](https://www.fire.org/news/2026-college-free-speech-rankings-americas-colleges-get-f-poor-free-speech-climate), published last September, surveyed more than 68,000 students across 257 college campuses in 2025. According to the findings, a record 34% of those students claimed that using violence to stop a campus speech is acceptable, at least in rare cases. This number jumped dramatically from the 18% of students who said the same when FIRE started asking the question in 2020.

More than two-thirds of students said the same thing about shouting down a speaker rather than letting them finish. And more than half of the students said that physically blocking the doors to an event is a reasonable way to prevent an event from happening. The report also noted that 166 of the 257 schools included in the survey had earned a failing grade for their speech climate. And, for the first time since the rankings began, most of the students said their school shouldn’t let any of the six controversial speakers onto campus – three from the left and three from the right.

What’s most compelling, though, is that this isn’t a one-party problem. FIRE’s own researchers even said so. “More students than ever think violence and chaos are acceptable alternatives to peaceful protest,” [Sean Stevens said](https://ocpathink.org/post/independent-journalism/kirks-death-comes-as-more-college-students-support-violence-over-speech), the organization’s chief research advisor. He went on to say that the pattern holds equally across the political spectrum. Students on the left and right find that intellectual disagreement is itself a threat.

FIRE’s report went public on Sept. 9, 2025. On the very next day, [Charlie Kirk](/2025/09/the-value-of-human-life-in-the-face-of-suffering/) was fatally shot at Utah Valley University, in the middle of doing the very thing the survey said was falling out of favor: engaging in intellectual discussion where there was disagreement.

We need to take a step back for a second and consider the implications of what this means. Approximately a third of future voters – doctors, lawyers, and elected officials – believe that it’s legitimate and necessary to physically stop someone from expressing an idea with which they disagree. To simply disagree with someone’s view is grounds for physical violence, according to these students. We are, in a very real way, scaffolding our own prison.

Eventually, we could find ourselves in a surveillance state that has nothing to do with the federal government. Instead, we’d be surrounded by our neighbors, those we’re supposed to trust, each of whom would play the warden.
