A friend of mine is working toward a literature degree and has lamented several times over the lack of book reading, as most assignments are excerpts or passages, with the occasional short story.

I was shocked by this news. But it made more sense when I saw a recent [Johns Hopkins study](https://www.thomasaquinas.edu/news/tac-sponsored-johns-hopkins-study-details-changes-sat) comparing the current SAT reading section to a couple from the 1980s. There’s been a huge slash in reading passage length (less than a third of what it was), a drop from about 60 vocabulary questions to 10, and a replacement of nearly all argumentative and persuasive texts to expository ones.

SAT scores are a significant determining factor in college admission, thus influencing much of our education system. Sadly, these changes offer no benefit and instead illustrate a literacy crisis. [Language is being undermined](/2026/05/why-reading-scores-keep-declining/), critical thinking weakened, and human expression is devolving. Given the looming world of AI, we should be especially cautious before discarding the staples of language education.

### **The Reel Effect on SAT Reading** 

[Short-form content is destroying our brains](/2025/11/gen-zs-media-literacy-is-dying-its-instagrams-fault/). We’re getting dumber and our attention spans are shot.

Even more frightening is how short-form content is so persuasive. People believe anything now as long as enough similar social media reels are pumped across their screens.

This happens because repetition eventually wears one down. Look at any totalitarian regime. Their propaganda departments were successful because they were relentless, not truthful.

It also happens because you can’t spot a bad argument if you don’t know how to make a good one.

Reels can be interesting and thought-provoking, but they’re not proofs. True arguments must be extensively researched, understood, and most importantly, contemplated. Various theories may make clever shorts, but they can never be comprehensive arguments.

Making a good argument, or even spotting one, takes time, study, and docility. It’s not as simple as laying out convincing information, or being convinced. It’s playing devil’s advocate and doing lots of research, writing, reading, and reflection. And as the SAT changes show, people aren’t being taught how to argue. The real effect of reducing argumentative texts in education will be a culture of people vehemently convicted by reels, increasingly primed to surrender their critical thinking.

### **Quality Over Quantity**

Summer for most kids means the pool; for me, it meant the library. One summer in high school, I challenged myself to read 50 books, upping it to 55 the next summer, and 60 the following one. While some decent fiction crossed my path, I also read a lot of trash novels. At worst they were obscene, at best, stupid.

My reading challenges taught me the importance of quality over quantity. It’s not hard to pound through 50 books when they’re cheap writing, but those books didn’t do much for me at all, and I remember almost nothing of them.

The ones I do remember were more challenging and took me longer to finish (even if they were short). Yet I still carry them with me, remembering the beautiful terror of contrapasso in Dante’s “Inferno,” the out-loud laughter of “The Importance of Being Earnest,” the heartbreak of “Tender is the Night,” and the dark humor of “Animal Farm.” In fact, “Crime and Punishment” left such an imprint on me it influenced my major and college choice.

Yes, there’s a decline in leisure reading, but even more concerning is what people _are_ reading. Bestseller lists are dominated by crime thrillers and romantasy – in essence, [gore and smut](/2025/05/bring-back-shaming/). This isn’t simply because “sex sells.” This is the result of not teaching people how to read true literature. Good books require perseverance and attention, and if students are only expected to read excerpts, they’ll never learn those skills.

### **The Potency of Vocabulary**

Vocabulary isn’t just for pretentious academics. Average people use vocabulary for deeper expression, connection, and understanding of the world, precisely because words signify reality. When we undermine vocabulary, we’re cutting our own experience of existence short.

We see this in the Russian language, which has more colors than ours. There are not actually more colors, just more words for them. An experiment was done examining their distinction of light blue and dark blue (each being different colors, with different names). The results showed Russians could identify them more quickly, meaning that language influenced their cognitive processing.

But this is not just true with colors. Psychological studies show that “if you can name it, you can tame it.” Putting emotions into words activates the prefrontal cortex, shifting the brain to problem-solving and regulating emotions. We need an expansive vocabulary for that, more than just “happy,” “sad,” and “confused.”

Language, by nature, shares. It is spoken to be heard and so implies an “other.” Weak vocabulary [weakens communication](/2025/02/getting-serious-about-americas-literacy-time-bomb/), which weakens connection. In some ways, this is the change to the SATs that frightens me the most. Reducing vocabulary strikes hard at the core of our humanity, including our ability to communicate with ourselves and to others.

As AI continues to make its foothold in the world, we must make sure we maintain ours, the footholds that make us uniquely human. Reading novels, having debates, and using a rich vocabulary – these are all beautiful gifts in the heritage of the human race, and we shouldn’t surrender them to become mere syntax for AI processing. Words will hold power as long as we know what they mean and how to wield them.
