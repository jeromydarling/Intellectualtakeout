You have likely seen the signs or bumper stickers proclaiming, “Jesus is the answer,” while driving down a highway in America, especially in the Midwest or the South. This slogan can be a good reminder for Christians, but when it is used to shut down questioning, or even attempt to convince non-Christians on its own, it can be unfruitful and even harmful.

Many Christians have a habit of [answering some very hard questions](/2026/09/problem-of-evil-good-god-suffering/) by simply regurgitating this slogan without knowing the idea behind it, yet the Bible does not claim to be an encyclopedia with every answer. If this slogan is interpreted to mean that Jesus automatically gives Christians knowledge, then someone may unduly doubt their faith if the Bible does not supply a clear and simple answer to their questions. The harm is in implying that no further inquiry is required once someone believes in Jesus.

The fourth century Church Father Augustine, however, describes knowledge in a way that leaves room for that inquiry while still allowing the slogan to be potentially true in a sense.

Writing in “The Teacher,” (De Magistro) Augustine and his son Adeodatus begin by asking what the purpose of speaking is, concluding that all words are signs that signify something other than themselves. Therefore, knowledge of a thing itself is always better than knowledge of its corresponding sign.

Herein lies a paradox. Words are not able to teach us anything new because if we already know the thing, the word simply reminds us of our knowledge of the thing. However, if we don’t know the thing, then the word tells us nothing except in relation to other words.

Therefore, when I’m stating truths, I don’t even teach the person who is looking upon these truths. He’s taught not by my words but by the things themselves made manifest within when God discloses them.

Slogans work in a similar way. They cannot illuminate the mind of another directly. Instead, to understand the truth of a thing, or to have knowledge of it, an individual’s mind must turn inward and be with the internal teacher. Augustine quotes Matthew 23:10, which says, “And do not be called teachers; for One is your Teacher, the Christ.” According to Augustine, if we appear to learn something from any human teacher, it is either only knowledge of words, or through the Truth being illuminated to us.

If people only meet the slogan of [Jesus as the answer](/2025/09/the-choice-before-us-chaos-or-christianity/), they miss the knowledge that the slogan doesn’t answer by itself. Not every question can be answered neatly. Some questions arise when someone realizes they may know the words but not the truth of them. This slogan can remind Christians of their knowledge of Jesus, but it cannot by itself lead someone to true knowledge. If the slogan is used as a slogan to articulate an idea imperfectly, Augustine has nothing to argue against. But if it is used as the final answer to every difficult question, it is being asked to do what no external word can do.
