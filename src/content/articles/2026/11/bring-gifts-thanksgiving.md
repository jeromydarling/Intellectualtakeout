Many Americans [rank](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/ranked-americas-favorite-holidays/) either Christmas or Thanksgiving as their favorite holiday.

While both occasions are oriented toward family and friends, the contrast is otherwise startling. Thanksgiving is humbler and quieter, a single day focused on gratitude and a special meal. Putting aside its religious roots and meaning, Christmas is a hoopla holiday, a season of decorations, Santa Claus, and parties. Unlike Thanksgiving, Christmas means shopping and selecting gifts, then wrapping them.

Whether we know it or not, Thanksgiving also obliges us to bring gifts to those tables loaded with turkey and trimmings. These presents don’t require money or fumbling around with paper, tape, and scissors, but they do demand care in their delivery, as well as keeping them front and center once they are opened.

I’m thinking of courtesy, kindness, patience, and some other virtues, all wrapped up and ribboned with [good cheer and laughter](/2026/10/gratitude-metronome-merry-heart/). These are the gifts we can bring to Thanksgiving Day, which is often easier said than done.

Before offering some tips on how to unwrap and share these Thanksgiving gifts, let me provide some credentials. For 35 years, I’ve either hosted or helped with large Thanksgiving meals. For 15 of those years, my wife and I played host to my five siblings, their spouses, and their children at our bed and breakfast. Following my wife’s death and the sale of our B&B, we’ve moved the festivities to my daughter’s home. Add in a few friends, and attendance this year will be right around 40 adults, teens, kids, and newborns.

What I’ve learned from these feasts may help others deal with the stresses and disappointments Thanksgiving can bring.

### **Flavor Your Expectations with Reality** This Thanksgiving

Keep your Norman Rockwell visions of the day and the meal if that’s how you roll. Rockwell’s famous painting of a Thanksgiving Day gathering gives viewers Grandma in her white apron carrying a platter with a juicy-looking brown turkey while Grandpa stands at her side in a coat and tie, likely ready to slice the bird. Around the table are family members and guests, all laughing at some joke.

Every November I entertain a similar vision of Thanksgiving, but after so many years of intimate involvement with this day I also ladle on a hearty helping of realistic expectations. Things will go awry. Some grandkid will scrape a knee in the driveway, someone will spill a gravy bowl, or someone will leave that evening with their feelings bruised by a chance remark.

Such things will happen, but don’t lower your expectations. Just temper them with some judicious common sense.

### **Exert Yourself**

Whether you’re hosting or attending, you have an obligation to bring your best self to this gala of gratitude. In her cookbook and celebration of Southern manners and customs, “Love Language of the South,” Alabama native Stacy Lyn Harris writes:

> Southerners are bred to be hospitable. Basically from birth, Southern girls are taught charm and manners, and Southern boys are taught to be gentlemen. Becky had best be adorning a smile, even if her shoes are giving her a blister. Johnny must give up his seat if a lady enters the room and there are no seats left, even if he had to wake early that morning and is bone tired. Nothing less will be tolerated.

Whether you’re 11 or 50 years old, whether you’re in Alabama, California, or Massachusetts, on this special day you have a social obligation to adorn yourself with a smile and some good humor, practice the courtesies, and show kindness to those around you.

This is the gift you give: the good side of yourself.

### **Look for Beauty and Mystery**

And this is the gift you receive. Amid the chaos of the meal preparation, or in the chatter around the table, look, really and truly look, at the faces of those around you. Look as if you’ve never seen them before. This study takes only a second, but quite often you’ll find beauty as you rarely see it, not physical but the beauty that shines in nearly all human beings when we make the effort to look for it.

In Thornton Wilder’s play “Our Town,” a deceased young woman who died in childbirth receives the chance to visit a day in her past. She chooses her 12th birthday, and is crushed to find how the people she loves “don’t have time to look at one another.” She then asks the Stage Manager, “Do any human beings ever realize life while they live it … every, every minute?”

“No,” the Stage Manager replies, and after a pause adds, “The saints and poets, maybe—they do some.”

We may not realize life, as Emily puts it, “every, every minute,” but we can, if we make the effort, take snapshots of its fullness and beauty. Will yourself to look with different eyes at those around you, and just for moment you’ll likely spot a mystery and luminescence in them commonly hidden by the shades of ordinary life.

To those readers [spending Thanksgiving](/2023/11/attitude-of-gratitude-thanksgiving-2023/) away from family, like two of my grandchildren this year who are in college, or for those alone, take command of your situation. Call a loved one. Volunteer in a soup kitchen. Go to a restaurant offering a special Thanksgiving meal and relish the conversations and laughter around you.

Bring your gifts with you and savor the beauty.
